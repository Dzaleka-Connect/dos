## What the research shows

The most cited evidence on mental health in Dzaleka is a study published in *Psychiatry Research* in 2022. Researchers screened 102 camp residents with the PHQ-9 questionnaire: 78% scored 10 or above, the threshold for probable depression, and 15% reported attempting suicide in the previous twelve months. For scale, the WHO estimates global depression prevalence at roughly 3.8%.

The study has limits that matter for reading it honestly. It is a single cross-sectional screening of 102 people in a camp of tens of thousands, without a described sampling strategy or a host-community comparison group — a screening signal, not a census. Even read cautiously, the signal is severe.

## The treatment gap

Measured distress far exceeds available care. Psychosocial services in the camp reach a small fraction of residents, and several barriers compound: language (clinicians often work in English or Chichewa while residents speak Kinyarwanda, Kirundi, Swahili, or Somali), stigma around the phrase "mental health," and distress that presents physically — headaches, fatigue, "msongo wa mawazo" ("too many thoughts") — rather than as a named condition.

## Who provides support

Jesuit Refugee Service runs the camp's main mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) programme, alongside the Dzaleka Health Centre's general services and faith- and community-based groups. Sports and arts organisations also function as informal psychosocial support: the Match Foundation has documented football-based mental health work with young people in the camp.

## Structural context

Research and reporting consistently tie the camp's mental health burden to structural conditions — encampment, overcrowding, and blocked livelihoods — documented in this encyclopedia's entries on law and policy. Clinical services address symptoms; the drivers are matters of policy.
