## Research

The most cited evidence on mental health in Dzaleka is a study published in [*Psychiatry Research*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36037741/) in 2022. Researchers screened 102 camp residents with the PHQ-9 questionnaire: 78% scored 10 or above, the threshold for probable depression, and 15% reported attempting suicide in the previous twelve months. For scale, the WHO estimates global depression prevalence at roughly 3.8%.

The study has limits that matter for reading it honestly. It is a single cross-sectional screening of 102 people in a camp of tens of thousands, without a described sampling strategy or a host-community comparison group — a screening signal, not a census. Even read cautiously, the signal is severe.

## The treatment gap

Measured distress far exceeds available care. Psychosocial services in the camp reach a small fraction of residents, and several barriers compound: language (clinicians often work in English or Chichewa while residents speak Kinyarwanda, Kirundi, Swahili, or Somali), stigma around the phrase "mental health," and distress that presents physically — headaches, fatigue, "msongo wa mawazo" ("too many thoughts") — rather than as a named condition.

## Who provides support

[Jesuit Refugee Service](/encyclopedia/jesuit-refugee-service) runs the camp's main mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) programme, alongside the [Dzaleka Health Centre](/encyclopedia/dzaleka-health-centre)'s general services and faith- and community-based groups. Sports and arts organisations also function as informal psychosocial support: the [Match Foundation](https://match-foundation.com/2025/03/20/mental-health-through-sports-at-dzaleka-camp/) has documented football-based mental health work with young people in the camp.

## Context

Research and reporting have linked the camp's mental health burden to structural conditions, including encampment, overcrowding, and restricted livelihoods, which are documented in this encyclopedia's entries on law and policy.
